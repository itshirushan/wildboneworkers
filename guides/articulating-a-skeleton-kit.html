Beginner's guide

How to articulate a skeleton kit

Turning a box of sorted bones into a standing skeleton is slow, absorbing work. Here's how to approach your first articulation, step by step.

Before you start

An unassembled kit contains every bone from a single animal: skull and jaw, the full spine, ribs, the shoulder and hip girdles, limbs and paws. Ours arrive cleaned, degreased and sorted by bone group, with each paw bagged separately. Keep those bags closed until you need them.

Give yourself time. A first mink skeleton usually takes several evenings, and a fox takes longer. Rushing is how small bones get lost or glued in the wrong order.

Tools and materials

  • Reference images. Find several anatomical diagrams and photos of articulated skeletons of the same species, from more than one angle.
  • Armature wire (stainless steel or galvanised) in a couple of thicknesses. You'll use it for the spine and for pinning joints.
  • Pin vise or small rotary tool with fine drill bits, for drilling pin holes.
  • Adhesives: a slow-setting two-part epoxy for strong joints, and a quick glue for tacking pieces in place while you check the pose.
  • Spacer material such as thin craft foam, felt or epoxy putty, to stand in for the cartilage between vertebrae.
  • Tweezers, a sorting tray or egg carton, and a dust mask for drilling.
  • A base, plus a sturdy upright rod if your pose needs support.

1. Lay everything out and identify it

Work on a light-coloured tray with a lip. Lay the bones out roughly in anatomical position: skull at one end, then the vertebrae in order, then ribs left and right, then the limbs. Match each piece against your references. Vertebrae change shape gradually along the spine, which helps you put them in order. Ribs get longer towards the middle of the chest, then shorter again.

Photograph the layout once you're happy with it. If anything gets knocked over later, that photo is your map.

2. Choose a pose

Decide on the final pose before you glue anything, because it changes how you bend the spine wire and set the limbs. A simple standing pose is the most forgiving for a first project. Study photos of the live animal for natural posture. Most mammals hold their neck and tail in a gentle curve, not a straight line.

3. Build the spine first

The spine is the backbone of the whole project, literally. Thread the vertebrae onto armature wire through the spinal canal, from the neck down through the tail, with a thin spacer between each one. Bend the wire into your chosen curve as you go. Once you're happy with it, fix the vertebrae with small dabs of glue.

4. Attach the ribs

Each rib meets the spine at a thoracic vertebra. Dry-fit the ribs in pairs from front to back, checking that the ribcage is symmetrical from above, then glue them. A strip of wire or card can stand in for the cartilage that joins the ribs at the breastbone, which helps hold the cage's shape.

5. Add the limbs and girdles

The pelvis connects firmly to the spine at the sacrum, so attach it next. The shoulder blades are different: in life they're held on by muscle, not bone, so they need to "float" on short wire supports against the ribcage. Pin each long bone to the next with a short length of wire in drilled holes. That's much stronger than glue alone. Keep checking the pose against your references.

6. Paws: the patient part

Paws are where beginners slow down, and that's normal. Open one bag at a time. Lay the small bones out in rows matching your reference diagram, then build each toe from the tip back towards the wrist or ankle. Tweezers and a quick-setting glue help here. Then attach the finished paw to the limb.

7. Skull, base and finishing

Seat the skull on the first neck vertebra, fit the jaw (a little epoxy putty at the joint lets you set it open or closed), and fix the skeleton to its base with the support rod. Let all the epoxy cure fully before moving the piece. Finally, dust it with a soft brush and find it a spot out of direct sunlight.

Tips from experienced articulators

  • Dry-fit everything twice. Glue is easy to add and hard to remove.
  • Work symmetrically. Do left and right together so both sides match.
  • Drill slowly with a fine bit. Bone can split if you force it, and always wear a mask, as bone dust shouldn't be inhaled.
  • Imperfect is fine. Every animal is a little different, and your first skeleton will teach you more than any guide.
